## Overview

Private equity firms running a buy-and-build strategy keep bolting smaller companies onto a platform company — and every add-on has to clear the lender. Someone has to prove the combined books stay under the credit agreement's leverage limit, and today that is a spreadsheet, built by hand, where one mis-mapped line quietly flows into every number after it.

Stochastic, built at Cal Hacks 13.0 (Hyphenate track), takes two sets of financial statements and the credit agreement and splits the work the way a real finance team would.

## How it works

1. **Read** the credit agreement and extract the limit, what counts as debt, and how EBITDA is adjusted — with a verbatim quote for every field.
2. **Map** every line of the target onto the platform's chart of accounts, with a confidence and a reason.
3. **Flag** only the genuinely unclear lines, and show the human what each answer would do to the result.
4. **Combine** the books and **test** leverage against the lender's limit using the agreement's own definitions.
5. **Review** the draft and hand it to a human controller to approve.

The model never produces a number. Every figure on screen comes from a code tool, and the gateway rejects any number no tool produced. Decisions the controller makes once become policies the next run applies automatically.

On the demo deal — Kewaunee Scientific's real acquisition of Nu Aire — one ambiguous "Financing liability" line swings leverage between **2.84x (in breach)** and **0.76x (plenty of room)**. That is the one line Stochastic asks a human about.

## What I built

- **Finance tool layer** in Python — consolidation, EBITDA, covenant debt, leverage, and the limit test — verified against a real credit agreement with a **435-test pytest suite**.
- **FastAPI routes** for CSV deal upload, SSE stage streaming, controller edits, the compliance certificate, and final workbook export, backed by Postgres/TimescaleDB on Tiger Cloud.

## Takeaway

Let the model handle judgment, keep it away from the math, and make a human sign the parts that carry weight. Building the deterministic tools the agents call was a lesson in how much trust correctness has to earn.
